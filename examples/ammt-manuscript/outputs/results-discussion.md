# Results and Discussion

## Geometry discrepancy after length calibration

Fitting the source parameter to the B length proxy did not remove the discrepancy in transverse fusion geometry. At B, the scalar conduction and equilibrium phase-change model gives a length of 359.2 µm against the experimental mean of 359 µm, but a width of 143.0 µm against 123.5 µm and a depth of 31.1 µm against 36 µm. The fitted length therefore coexists with a wide, shallow fusion envelope. Width and depth were excluded from the calibration objective and provide distinct post-calibration diagnostics. The effective source parameter, η = 0.28905, was held fixed for the subsequent condition comparisons and constitutive contrasts. [N1]

The discrepancy becomes more pronounced at C. Model length, width and depth are 320.6, 129.0 and 22.0 µm, respectively, compared with experimental means of 370, 106 and 29.6 µm. At A, the corresponding model values are 309.9, 153.4 and 40.5 µm, against 300, 147.9 and 42.5 µm. Dividing signed differences by the separately sourced experimental expanded uncertainty gives approximately +0.83, +0.85 and −0.45 at A; +0.007, +3.10 and −1.23 at B; and −1.52, +5.14 and −2.32 at C. Thus all three A differences lie within that measurement-uncertainty scale, whereas B width/depth and all three C differences lie outside it. These descriptive ratios use experimental uncertainty alone and are neither significance tests nor combined model–experiment uncertainty scores. [N1, N2]

The data roles and observation operators qualify this comparison. A and C are retrospective, nonblind condition comparisons: their public targets were known before the study, although they did not enter the scalar calibration objective. Experimental length is obtained from thermography through radiance calibration and a derivative-based solidification location. Model length instead uses two solidus crossings of the reconstructed surface centerline. Experimental width/depth are metallographic class means, whereas model width/depth describe the source-connected envelope reached during material passage through the stationary field. Length and cross-section targets therefore do not describe a simultaneous three-dimensional measurement of one track. The fitted η absorbs source, model and observation-operator differences; it is not an independently identified absorptivity. [N2]

## Operating-condition response and passage time

Increasing speed from B to C at unchanged power produces contrasting axial and transverse responses. The model length decreases by 38.5 µm, or 10.7%, while the experimental mean increases by 11 µm, or 3.1%. The latter nominal change is smaller than the 38.9 µm quadrature scale obtained from the two expanded uncertainties if zero cross-condition correlation is assumed. The opposite nominal signs identify a response discrepancy, but do not establish a statistically resolved experimental length increase. Model width falls by 9.8% and depth by 29.2%; the experimental class means fall by 14.2% and 17.8%, respectively. These unpropagated percentage contrasts describe the available means rather than uncertainty-qualified response laws. [N3]

The transverse imbalance also appears in the width-to-depth ratios. Model ratios increase from 3.79 at A to 4.60 at B and 5.85 at C, while ratios of experimental means are approximately 3.48, 3.43 and 3.58. For the fixed-power B-to-C comparison, increasing speed preferentially reduces modeled penetration relative to lateral extent. This trend is consistent with the increasingly wide, shallow discrepancy at B and C. Since the experimental ratios combine class means and lack propagated covariance, they remain descriptive shape indicators. [N3]

Passage time distinguishes a spatial phase interval from the thermal history implied by translation. With a beam radius of 85 µm, the characteristic beam passage scale, w/v, is 212.5, 106.3 and 70.8 µs for A, B and C. The model absorbed line energies, ηP/v, are approximately 99.7, 64.7 and 43.2 J/m. The rear centerline liquidus-to-solidus spans are 48.3, 80.3 and 80.8 µm. Dividing these spans by scan speed gives phase-interval passage times of 120.7, 100.3 and 67.3 µs. Consequently, the nearly equal B/C spatial spans correspond to substantially different passage times. Coordinate enthalpy transport represents stationary material traversing the laser frame; it does not represent liquid circulation. [N3]

Using the prescribed 60 °C phase interval, these passage times imply model centerline mean cooling rates of approximately 0.50, 0.60 and 0.89 MK/s. They characterize the rear liquidus-to-solidus interval of the assumed quasi-steady solution. They are not the experimental cooling-rate observable, and the retained experimental source labels its cooling-rate examples unsuitable as reference or calibration data. The useful result is the model distinction between tail extent and cooling duration: similar tail spans need not imply similar histories when scan speed changes. [N3]

## Competition between high-temperature closures

The fixed-B four-corner contrast separates conductivity continuation from sensible heat-capacity continuation without refitting η. Each property is either linearly extrapolated beyond its own table endpoint (L) or held at its final value (H); the first letter denotes conductivity and the second heat capacity. All material at or above the solidus lies beyond both tabulated endpoints. These alternatives therefore test constitutive closures in the thermally active region, rather than competing measurements of liquid properties. The six unique production calculations comprise A/B/C and the four B corners, with baseline B also serving as LL. [N4]

Holding conductivity while retaining linear heat capacity increases length from 359.2 µm at LL to 402.4 µm at HL. Holding heat capacity alone reduces length to 353.0 µm at LH. Holding both gives 393.4 µm at HH, a joint increase of 34.3 µm. Averaging each factor's conditional differences across the other factor gives a conductivity effect of +41.8 µm and a heat-capacity effect of −7.5 µm. The difference-of-differences, HH − HL − LH + LL, is −2.8 µm. Conductivity continuation therefore dominates the axial response, while heat-capacity continuation partly opposes it. These are deterministic finite contrasts at B, without statistical replication or demonstrated cross-speed transfer. [N4]

Boundary locations explain the length response more specifically. For the conductivity-only change, the rear solidus and liquidus move backward by 42.5 and 43.5 µm, whereas the front solidus moves forward by about 0.7 µm. For the heat-capacity-only change, the rear boundaries move forward by 6.2 and 6.5 µm. Across the four corners, the rear phase span remains approximately 79–81 µm and its passage time approximately 99–101 µs. The large axial response chiefly reflects common translation of the rear boundaries, rather than marked broadening of the phase interval. Fine differences within these interpolated spans have not been established by branch-specific convergence. [N4]

Matched-temperature properties support this model-internal interpretation. At 1320 °C, holding conductivity reduces k from 32.51 to 25.2 W/(m K), approximately 22.5%, whereas holding heat capacity reduces latent-inclusive effective heat capacity from 5387.8 to 5336.7 J/(kg K), approximately 0.95%. The large latent contribution buffers the sensible storage change within the phase interval. The mushy-region median axial face Péclet number increases from 4.44 at LL to 5.77 at HL, consistent with reduced modeled diffusion relative to coordinate transport; LH gives 4.39. This diagnostic explains a response within the conduction–enthalpy formulation without identifying a liquid-flow mechanism. [N4]

The closure alternatives do not repair the transverse discrepancy. All four corners overpredict B width and underpredict its depth. The joint HH change increases width by about 0.7 µm and reduces depth by about 0.3 µm. That small net depth response conceals opposing average effects: approximately −0.9 µm from conductivity and +0.6 µm from heat capacity. Large axial sensitivity consequently coexists with persistent transverse bias. A better length match alone would not identify an adequate transport closure. [N4]

## Numerical scope and implications for model development

The retained numerical checks support interpretation of the major tail contrast while limiting smaller-effect rankings. All six production runs satisfy their recorded local residual, global balance and full-correction criteria. At frozen η, axial refinement changes B length by approximately 0.36 µm, much less than the 34–43 µm continuation response. The constant-property analytical benchmark gives geometry differences below 0.5%, but addresses a simpler problem. The retained refinement and domain contrasts are observed sensitivities, not certified absolute discretization bounds or full three-dimensional asymptotic convergence. The approximately 0.09 µm average conductivity effect on width therefore warrants less confidence than the large axial contrast. [N5]

The residual pattern leaves several physical and observational explanations in competition. The reconstructed circular Gaussian does not replay the measured two-dimensional beam distribution. High-temperature property and phase closures, unreplayed thermographic operators, and omitted directional liquid transport could each contribute. The supplied original numerical source exhibits a narrow, deep isotropic bias, opposite to the present wide, shallow bias, and subsequently applies fitted directional conductivity factors. This precedent motivates examining effective directional transport, but its coefficients cannot be transferred as material constants between different source and closure choices. The present factorial isolates continuation choices within one model; it does not distinguish these competing explanations experimentally. [N6]

The supported contribution is consequently a diagnosis of geometry discrepancy and closure competition in a length-calibrated conduction/phase-change model. Internal temperature peaks remain unvalidated model states in a formulation without evaporation, liquid flow or free-surface response. Stronger prediction claims require operator-aligned observations, independent conditions and propagated calibration, input and numerical uncertainty. A focused next mechanism contrast should distinguish beam/operator effects from directional transport before introducing further fitted parameters; a targeted branch refinement is the cheaper next check for any proposed submicrometre effect. [N5, N6]
