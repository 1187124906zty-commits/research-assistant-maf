# Scientific objects and sentence continuity

Apply this compact method to the assigned passages. Its reader-expectation basis is the Duke Graduate School Scientific Writing Resource, Lessons 1–2; source identity and comparator checks are this project's scientific editorial method. See [cohesion sources](cohesion-source-ledger.md) and the existing [source-use guidance](source-use.md). These are adaptable judgments, not rules requiring every sentence to repeat the same noun.

## Establish the object before polishing its name

For an unclear noun or comparison, first determine the actual object, property/observable, material/population or model, condition and source. Does it denote a measurement, calculation, theoretical reference, fitted parameter, local definition or interpretation? Read the originating passage or saved evidence. A vaguely similar title cannot supply the identity. Keep a short term map only for consequential or disputed terms; do not annotate every familiar word.

Name comparisons by what is compared. Replace an undefined “reference mean” with the relevant measurement and population; identify a theoretical benchmark by its assumptions, and a numerical variant by the function or input that changed. Say which baseline, which retained conditions and which response establish a claimed effect. Avoid substituting “controlled”, “independent”, “validated” or “improved” for this information. Those words require their own design/evidence support.

For inherited methods, trace predecessor object and result → its specific unresolved aspect → the present modification/comparison → the evidence that answers it. Distinguish the source author's stated limitation from a limitation inferred here. Source naming is authoritative for the source's method, but a locally defined mathematical rule must be identified and defined as such. Do not invent a literature-established term or claim an improved model when the actual contribution is a sensitivity or interpretation.

A new name/acronym is useful only when readers need to reuse a distinct, explicitly defined entity and it reduces effort. Check closest literature for collisions and meaning. A descriptive name is often enough. Define the long form first; keep it consistent across title/abstract/body. Naming cannot establish novelty, causality or effectiveness.

## Repair the relation, then the wording

For a difficult sentence, state its plain grammatical core: **object → action/relation → affected object**. Include the condition/comparator that changes its meaning. Check what each modifier modifies and what each pronoun refers to. An abstract noun chain can hide a missing relation even when each word is grammatical.

Read the sentence with its predecessor. Put a recognizable object or premise near the beginning when helpful, then develop what is new. Introduce a necessary new object through its role in the already stated problem, before relying on its name or symbol. Preserve a consistent topical thread; a change of grammatical subject should have a reason the reader can follow. Citation authors are appropriate subjects when comparing what they did; use the shared scientific object when synthesizing their findings.

For a difficult paragraph, temporarily mark each sentence's grammatical subject, main action/relation, old premise and new item. Read the subject sequence to see whether the topic is retained or deliberately advanced. For “this”, “it”, “their” or “the latter”, identify a unique antecedent; if several qualify, use a named noun or restructure. Remove these diagnostic annotations from manuscript prose.

**Original hypothetical example:** after establishing that a model underpredicts penetration, “The source factor reproduced length better” hides both the factor's function and the comparison. If the actual design supports it: “The source factor scales the energy delivered to the substrate. Fitting that factor to the measured surface length leaves the predicted penetration below the metallographic depth.” The text introduces the parameter through its role, specifies the fit and states the remaining mismatch. Do not use the example when fitting or the mismatch did not occur.

At each consequential sentence/paragraph boundary identify the relation: prerequisite, narrowing, evidence, contrast, design rationale, consequence or unresolved interpretation. Write the missing premise or move the material when needed. Then select a connector that expresses that relation. “Therefore” needs an inference; “however” needs a contrast; “furthermore” cannot connect unrelated objects. Repetition, synonyms or connectors cannot repair an absent scientific link.

For a paragraph, identify its controlling point, supporting evidence and the recognition it leaves for the next paragraph. Not every paragraph needs a gap or an identical closure sentence. For Methods, connect the scientific need to the design and what its definitions make assessable. For Results, connect the defined comparison to the observed response and what it answers. Source attribution belongs with the borrowed assumption, equation or finding rather than as decoration after a vague generality.

Test both levels: **cohesion**, whether adjacent sentences connect through recognizable information; and **coherence**, whether their combined reasoning develops the paragraph's controlling point. A smoothly linked paragraph can still drift away from the paper's question. Repair misplaced evidence or the controlling point as well as local links.

## Return and review

Return the complete assigned prose and a short note on consequential definitions, changed relations or unresolved source questions. Before accepting it, a reviewer should be able to identify the objects and comparator, explain why each new object enters, and restate the relation across the weakest transitions. Locate any failure and its smallest meaningful repair. Fluent sentences alone do not satisfy this check; mechanical old→new order is not mandatory when a different construction communicates the science clearly.
